# Reusable rockets

For most of the space age, rockets were used once and thrown away, like building a new airplane
for every flight. Reusing them is the biggest change in the cost of reaching orbit since
[[Sputnik 1]].

## A short history

- The [[Space Shuttle]] (1981–2011) was partly reusable: the orbiter and boosters flew again, but
  refurbishing them took months and cost far more than planned.
- **November 23, 2015:** Blue Origin's suborbital New Shepard landed vertically after reaching
  space.
- **December 21, 2015:** a SpaceX Falcon 9 first stage landed back at Cape Canaveral after
  launching satellites to orbit: the first landing of an orbital-class booster.
- **March 30, 2017:** a Falcon 9 booster flew to orbit for the second time.
- **October 13, 2024:** SpaceX caught a returning Super Heavy booster with the arms of its launch
  tower.

Boosters now routinely fly many times each, and crews and cargo reach the
[[International Space Station]] on reused rockets.

> [!question] Why land a rocket at all?
> The first stage is the most expensive part of a rocket, and it only flies for a few minutes.
> Landing it and flying it again spreads its cost over many launches.

## What's next

Fully reusable rockets, where every stage comes back, are the goal of several companies. The
landers planned for the [[Artemis program]] depend on them.
